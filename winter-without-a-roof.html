The Problem

Kazakhstan experiences extremely cold winters, particularly in its northern and central regions. As someone who grew up in Karaganda, I have often wondered what happens to people who have nowhere to sleep when temperatures fall far below freezing. Most of us can return to warm homes. People sleeping rough may have no heating, adequate protection, or safe place to spend the night. In winter, homelessness can become a matter of survival.

Prolonged cold exposure can cause hypothermia, frostbite, and death. This project therefore focuses on people sleeping rough or otherwise lacking safe overnight accommodation during dangerous winter conditions. Homelessness also includes people in temporary or insecure housing, but not everyone experiencing housing insecurity requires emergency winter shelter.

Kazakhstan formally recognizes homelessness as a condition requiring social protection. Article 133 of the Social Code identifies homelessness as grounds for receiving special social services, while Article 139 allows people requiring assistance because of homelessness to receive services regardless of their place of residence (Social Code of Kazakhstan, 2023). The institutional system includes social adaptation centres, overnight accommodation, and mobile social patrols. In Karaganda, for example, a new centre opened in November 2024 to provide accommodation, food, clothing, street outreach, and help restoring identification documents (Karaganda Regional Akimat, 2024).

This legal recognition matters because a person without a registered address should not become ineligible for help precisely because they lack an address. It shifts the policy question away from whether the state has any responsibility and toward how that responsibility is carried out during periods of immediate physical danger.

Yet the existence of a centre does not establish that emergency shelter is accessible. The Bureau of National Statistics recorded 34 temporary-stay organizations in 2024 and 36 at the end of 2025 (Bureau of National Statistics, 2025). These counts do not show how many beds are available, how many people need them, or whether capacity is located where winter demand is greatest.

A person may live in the same region as a shelter but still be unable to reach it, gain admission, or find an available place. The central problem is therefore whether the existing system can identify people at immediate risk from cold and provide accommodation where and when they need it.

The Social Science Question

To what extent do emergency winter shelter services meet the needs of people sleeping rough in northern and central Kazakhstan, and how do gaps in available information complicate the assessment of their accessibility?

Background and Data

Kazakhstan's public data describe parts of the shelter system, but no source provides a complete measure of winter need. The Bureau of National Statistics counts organizations providing special social services. The Ministry of Labour and Social Protection monitors licensing and service provision. Akimats publish local information about adaptation centres, overnight stays, and outreach. These sources count different things: facilities, licensed centres, service recipients, visits, or outreach contacts.

From 2021 to 2025, the total number of special social service organizations grew by 30.5%, while temporary-stay organizations grew by 16.1%. This shows institutional expansion, but an organization is not a bed. The data do not report simultaneous capacity, occupancy, admissions refused for lack of space, or additional places opened during severe cold.

The Ministry reported 25 licensed adaptation centres and 3,216 service recipients as of August 31, 2026 (Ministry of Labour and Social Protection, 2026). This does not necessarily contradict the Bureau's 36 temporary-stay organizations: the agencies use different classifications and reporting dates, and the Ministry does not specify the period represented by the recipient count.

Local reports add detail but remain difficult to compare. Kostanay Region reported four resocialization and adaptation centres and 1,704 recipients, including 1,114 overnight-service users. Kokshetau reported 147 overnight users as of September 1, 2024, including repeat visits. Outreach teams contacted 124 people described as engaged in vagrancy or begging; 47 declined special social services. The report does not explain whether they needed shelter or why they declined it.

International indicators provide context rather than a measure of shelter demand. The World Bank documents large seasonal temperature differences across Kazakhstan, and the World Health Organization identifies hypothermia and frostbite as serious consequences of prolonged cold. UNDP's Digital Family Card shows that Kazakhstan can integrate administrative data to identify vulnerable households. Public documentation, however, does not establish whether people sleeping rough, especially those without stable addresses or documents, are reliably represented in that system.

Academic research reveals another selection problem. Uasheva and Sagyn (2026) surveyed 50 people already receiving assistance at Astana's Demeu centre. Fifty-two percent identified permanent accommodation or a hostel as their most needed form of help. The study documents serious need among service users, but excludes people who never reached the centre, could not access it, or avoided it. Some reported percentages also conflict with respondent counts, so individual figures require caution.

Where the Data Goes Silent

The public record lacks the denominator needed to assess coverage: a verified count of people requiring emergency shelter on a given winter night. It also lacks a common dataset connecting shelter location, bed capacity, occupancy, unmet requests, outreach contacts, and transport access. Annual admissions cannot show simultaneous capacity, and service users are not always unique individuals.

The 2023 service standards provide winter overnight accommodation from 18:00 to 10:00 for a specified category of users, without hot meals. They do not show what happens during dangerously cold daytime hours or how rules operate in practice. Public sources rarely report waiting times, refusals, full facilities, or temporary winter expansion. Kazakhstan can count parts of its institutional response, but it cannot yet determine how much emergency need remains unmet.

Current State of Research

Research relevant to winter shelter access follows four approaches.

Definitions and measurement. Somerville (2013) argues that homelessness involves more than the absence of a roof; it also concerns security, privacy, relationships, and belonging. Reviews by Deleu and colleagues (2023) and Van Loon and colleagues (2026) show that definitions determine who becomes visible. Narrow definitions are easier to count but may exclude hidden homelessness. This project uses a narrower operational population: people sleeping rough or lacking safe overnight accommodation during dangerous cold.

Cold exposure and health. Zhang and colleagues (2019) examined 97 hypothermic events among people experiencing homelessness in Toronto, including 18 deaths. The odds of an event rose by about 64% for every 5°C decrease in minimum temperature, yet 72% occurred above −15°C. A 2025 systematic review also found consistent evidence of elevated cold-related harm, but weaker evidence about which interventions prevent it. Canadian thresholds cannot simply be transferred to Kazakhstan, but the studies show why winter response should not wait for record-breaking cold.

Yao, Mema, and Henderson (2026) reached a related conclusion using linked health records in British Columbia. Mortality risk increased below 0°C among people with a history of homelessness. Their data could not show whether those individuals were sleeping outdoors at the time, so the finding demonstrates vulnerability rather than the direct protective effect of a shelter bed.

Shelter accessibility. Tsai, Haley, and Kinney (2024) reviewed self-reported reasons for remaining unsheltered. Recurring barriers included safety concerns, strict rules, poor conditions, distrust, disability access, and restrictions affecting families or pets. Bansal and colleagues (2025) found that shelter restrictions sometimes returned people to unsafe environments. These findings identify possible mechanisms, not verified barriers in Kazakhstan. They nevertheless show why a nominally available bed may not be practically accessible.

They also expose a real institutional tradeoff. Shelters need rules that protect residents and staff, yet the same rules can exclude people who have nowhere else to go. The question is whether admission practices are proportionate, documented, and designed for people facing dangerous conditions.

Kazakhstan and post-Soviet experience. Uasheva and Sagyn (2026) document unemployment, family conflict, substance dependency, missing documents, and isolation among Demeu service users. Kuziner's interviews in St. Petersburg show that people also survive in abandoned buildings, informal accommodation, and exploitative labour houses. Women may rely on relationships for temporary accommodation and remain hidden in official counts. These studies explain exclusion and survival, but neither measures winter capacity, distance to services, or unmet demand in Kazakhstan.

The methods also shape what each study can claim. Administrative records are broad but miss people outside the service system. Small surveys and interviews reveal experiences but cannot estimate a population. Health studies quantify risk but rarely identify which shelter policy prevented an injury or death. A credible assessment must combine these forms of evidence without treating any one of them as complete.

The Research Gap

Existing research establishes that homelessness is hard to count, cold exposure is dangerous, and shelter availability does not guarantee access. What is missing is an integrated assessment connecting emergency bed availability, geographical accessibility, seasonal demand, and people who remain outside formal services in northern and central Kazakhstan.

The AI Assisted Solution and What It Would Cost

The Proposal WinterSafe Kazakhstan

I propose a limited three-city pilot in Astana, Karaganda, and Kostanay. WinterSafe would combine a shared shelter-occupancy platform with short-term demand forecasting. It would not predict which individuals are likely to become homeless or rank people by deservingness.

First, participating shelters would report total capacity, occupied beds, available places, and relevant admission conditions. Social workers could see current vacancies without calling every institution. This is an ordinary information system and should be built before any AI component.

Second, an interpretable forecasting model would use historical occupancy, requests for assistance, outreach contacts, and weather forecasts to estimate emergency demand 24–72 hours ahead. If a cold spell coincided with high occupancy, the system could warn coordinators that extra capacity or transport might be needed. More complex models would be considered only if they performed better than simple statistical forecasting.

Third, the platform would suggest operational responses: contact another facility, arrange transport, extend outreach, or open temporary accommodation. A social worker or municipal coordinator would decide what action to take. No person would need a smartphone or identification document merely to be included in aggregate demand estimates, and nobody would be denied help because of an algorithmic score.

Two Real Cases

Los Angeles County. The Homelessness Prevention Unit combines predictive modelling with case managers and financial assistance. A 2025 county evaluation compared 335 participants with 1,285 eligible non-participants and reported that participants were 71% less likely to enter a shelter or have street-outreach contact during the following 18 months. The comparison was not randomized, and the intervention included substantial human support. The result therefore cannot be attributed to AI alone. Its lesson for WinterSafe is institutional: prediction is useful only when staff have resources to act.

Maidstone and Kent. Maidstone Borough Council used AI alerts to identify residents at risk of homelessness. A 2024 Equality and Human Rights Commission case study reported that 68% of residents offered help after an alert engaged with the intervention; homelessness was reportedly prevented for 98% of those who engaged. The council also reported 27% more preventions than under its standard approach. These figures came from operational data, not a randomized trial, and concern prevention rather than winter shelter allocation. They show the potential of combined data and human assistance, while leaving WinterSafe's effectiveness unproven.

Environmental. WinterSafe would use a database and a small forecasting model, not a generative model trained from scratch. If its computing load equalled two 120-watt servers with a data-centre power usage effectiveness of 1.5, annual electricity demand would be about 3.2 MWh. At an illustrative emissions factor of 0.7–0.9 kg CO₂ per kWh, operational emissions would be about 2.2–2.8 tonnes of CO₂. These are modelling assumptions, not provider measurements. Kazakhstan's electricity balance is reported by KEGOC; broader research also warns that AI energy figures depend heavily on model and infrastructure choices (Luccioni, Trevelin, and Mitchell, 2024). The pilot should use existing Kazakhstan-based hosting, avoid large models, and publish actual energy and water use where available.

Water use is harder to estimate because it depends on the hosting facility's cooling system and electricity supply. A hypothetical direct cooling range of 0.2–2 litres per kWh of IT electricity would imply roughly 420–4,200 litres annually, excluding water used to generate electricity. The range is too uncertain to present as a forecast, but it identifies information that procurement should require from the provider; research on AI water footprints similarly emphasizes infrastructure-specific measurement (Li et al., 2025).

Economic. The ₸59.6 million estimate includes data preparation, staff, security, evaluation, and limited transport support. It excludes new shelters, long-term housing, and permanent service expansion. The same money could fund outreach, transport, staffing, or direct assistance. Policymakers should therefore compare WinterSafe with a non-AI occupancy dashboard and telephone coordination. The relevant question is whether forecasting produces enough additional benefit to justify its incremental cost.

Labour. Automation would not remove the need for social workers. Staff would still verify vacancies, correct records, investigate errors, build trust, and arrange assistance. Research on AI labour also shows that apparently automated systems depend on extensive human work (Tubaro, Casilli, and Coville, 2020). New reporting can become unpaid work if responsibilities are simply added to existing jobs. The pilot therefore budgets for coordinators and training. It must not be used to cut social-worker positions or evaluate staff solely through algorithmic indicators.

Political and governance. Information about sleeping locations, outreach contacts, and service use could become a surveillance tool. WinterSafe should be legally restricted to social assistance. Law-enforcement agencies should not receive routine access to identifiable shelter data, and public reports should contain aggregated statistics rather than the precise locations of informal sleeping sites. Kazakhstan's 2025 AI law, effective January 18, 2026, creates risk, transparency, and accountability requirements. The system would need formal legal assessment, domestic data storage, access controls, and an auditable chain of responsibility.

The restriction should be practical, not merely stated in policy. Access should be role-based and logged; data sharing outside the shelter system should require a documented lawful basis; and an independent auditor should examine whether actual use matches the stated social-assistance purpose. The municipality must also be able to suspend the system if the vendor cannot explain errors or meet security requirements.

Data ethics. A model trained on shelter admissions may learn where institutions already record activity, not where need is greatest. Areas with weak outreach may appear to have low demand. WinterSafe should distinguish recorded demand from estimated unmet need, display uncertainty, and flag areas with insufficient observations. It should mainly process non-identifying operational data. Personal information needed for a referral should be handled separately, and people must retain access to a human worker and a way to correct errors. Consultation with people who have experienced homelessness should be voluntary, compensated, and protected. These safeguards follow the human-rights and accountability direction of the UNESCO AI ethics recommendation and the OECD AI principles, while the applicable Kazakhstan law would still govern the pilot.

Emergency help should not depend on agreeing to optional data collection. Where the law permits service provision on another lawful basis, refusal to share extra personal information should not block accommodation. People should be told when AI informs administration, what information is used, and who can correct a harmful mistake.

How the Pilot Would Be Tested

WinterSafe must demonstrate that AI adds value beyond ordinary digital coordination. The evaluation should compare it with a shared occupancy dashboard and human-led referrals without predictive AI. Both systems would be assessed using the accuracy of vacancy reports, time needed to locate accommodation, unsuccessful referrals, time from outreach contact to admission, and requests that could not be accommodated. Forecast accuracy should be reported separately.

Higher recorded demand would not necessarily mean failure; better outreach may reveal people previously absent from official data. An independent evaluator should compare results across the three cities. If forecasting does not improve real service outcomes over the non-AI baseline, the AI component should be removed while useful administrative improvements are retained.

The Verdict

Yes But Only Under Five Conditions

Kazakhstan should consider a limited WinterSafe pilot, but the evidence supports only a conditional yes. International cases show that predictive systems can support earlier intervention when institutions also provide skilled workers and practical assistance. They do not prove that AI will improve winter shelter allocation in Kazakhstan. The pilot would also cost about ₸59.6 million while creating privacy, labour, environmental, and governance obligations.

  1. Build the information system first. Shelters must report capacity, occupancy, admissions, and unmet requests consistently. Introduce forecasting only if it improves coordination beyond a conventional database.
  2. Require independent comparison. Test AI-assisted coordination against the same platform without predictive AI. Measure successful referrals, waiting times, and requests that could not be accommodated, not prediction accuracy alone.
  3. Prevent surveillance. Minimize personal data, restrict use to social assistance, protect domestic storage, and keep human social workers responsible for individual decisions.
  4. Protect workers and publish costs. Use competitive procurement, disclose contracts and operating costs, and pay staff for new coordination and data-maintenance duties.
  5. Keep direct assistance central. Continue funding shelter places, emergency transport, outreach, food, and basic services. A forecast can reveal a shortage, but it cannot create a bed.

If these conditions cannot be met, Kazakhstan should prioritize conventional services. WinterSafe should be judged by whether more people can find a safe place to sleep during dangerous cold. If AI cannot improve that outcome, public money should go to direct assistance instead.

This verdict authorizes a comparison, not a permanent system. A successful dashboard would not by itself justify predictive modelling, and an accurate forecast would not count as success if people still could not reach an available bed.

What I accepted. I retained the distinction between counting organizations and measuring actual shelter access; the organization of the literature by research approach; a limited three-city pilot; comparison with a non-AI occupancy system; and a verdict based on service outcomes rather than forecasting accuracy alone.

What I rejected. I rejected the initial air-pollution topic because it did not reflect my interests. ChatGPT and I then explored why Kazakhstanis may avoid psychological and psychiatric services, including explanations extending beyond stigma to cultural helplessness, executive dysfunction and the Soviet psychiatric legacy. We decided not to pursue it because the causal argument and intervention were too broad for this project. I also rejected an early shortened website draft because it removed in-text evidence links and graphs. Finally, I rejected treating the national facility count as proof that shelter capacity is adequate or accessible.

My own work. I selected winter homelessness based on my experience growing up in Karaganda, defined the research question and geographical scope, supplied and reviewed sources, challenged interpretations, and made the final decisions about the proposal, evidence and conditional verdict.
